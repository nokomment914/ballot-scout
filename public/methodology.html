How Ballot Scout works

Ballot Scout shows the public record of every candidate on your ballot using official and well-documented public databases. This page lists every source, the rules we follow, and what’s missing.

Impartiality rules

These rules are enforced in the code, not just promised here.

  • Same fields for everyone. Every candidate gets the same “At a glance” box and the same six sections (Positions on your issues, Votes on your issues, Bills they sponsored, What their policies did, Money, Sources) in the same layout, whether or not they have records.
  • Neutral order. Candidates appear in official ballot order when our data gives a position for every candidate in a contest. Otherwise they’re listed alphabetically by last name. Party is never used to order anything.
  • No judgments. No grades, scores, ratings, endorsements, “alignment” percentages, or adjectives about candidates. Party lines, votes and money are all styled identically, with no party colors.
  • Quoted, or paraphrased by fixed rules. Official text is quoted verbatim. Position summaries are short paraphrases of the candidate’s own statements, written to the same rules for every candidate, each linked to where the candidate said it. Quoted official text includes Congressional Research Service bill summaries, Congressional Budget Office estimate titles, New York sponsor memos and the Governor’s messages, each with a link to the source; long text is shortened only by cutting it off with an ellipsis.
  • Whole races, not single candidates. Positions for a race are shown only when every candidate in it has been fully researched. Until then, no one’s are, so a candidate’s positions never appear next to an opponent who hasn’t been researched yet.
  • Everything is linked. Each vote links to its official roll call or bill page, each bill to its official page, and each dollar figure to the FEC. Each section shows its source and when it was last updated.
  • No record isn’t a mark against anyone. Candidates who haven’t served in a legislature are labeled “No legislative voting record in these databases.” Executive officeholders are labeled “Executive office: no voting record,” with documented official actions shown where a database exists (the Governor’s bill signatures and vetoes). Judicial candidates are labeled “Judicial races have no voting records.” Candidates for city, county, town and village offices are labeled “City, county, town and village records aren’t covered by this guide yet.”
  • When unsure, we show nothing. If a name can’t be matched to exactly one database record, we don’t link it, so no one is shown someone else’s record.

Where this guide covers

The researched ballot file covers the November 3, 2026 general election in New York City (all five boroughs), Westchester County, and Long Island (Nassau and Suffolk counties):

  • Statewide: Governor and Lieutenant Governor, Comptroller and Attorney General. There is no U.S. Senate race in New York in 2026.
  • Congress and the Legislature: U.S. House NY-1 through NY-17, every State Senate and Assembly district with territory in these counties, and the Supreme Court races in the 1st, 2nd, 9th, 10th, 11th, 12th and 13th Judicial Districts. Names and party lines follow the NYS Board of Elections certification (amended October 7, 2026), checked against each county Board of Elections’ list. Where a county lists a candidate under a different name, both are shown.
  • Local offices outside New York City, from each county Board of Elections’ 2026 candidate list:
    • Westchester: all 17 County Legislature seats; town supervisor, clerk, highway superintendent, justice and unexpired council seats where they’re up; City Court judges in Mount Vernon and Yonkers and unexpired council seats in Rye and White Plains; and offices in the 15 villages that vote in November.
    • Nassau: all 19 County Legislature seats, the supervisors of Hempstead, North Hempstead and Oyster Bay, the Hempstead and Oyster Bay town clerks, Family Court, and the 2nd, 3rd and 4th District Court districts.
    • Suffolk: County Clerk and Comptroller, County Court, Family Court, the 3rd, 5th and 6th District Court districts, and town offices in East Hampton, Riverhead, Shelter Island, Smithtown and Southampton.

Why these local offices: New York’s Even Year Election Law (Laws of 2023, chapter 741), upheld by the Court of Appeals in 2025, moves most town and county offices to even-numbered years. Officials elected in 2025 to two-year terms got one-year terms, so those seats are on this ballot. Four-year offices elected in 2025, such as the Westchester and Nassau County Executives, now run to 2028. The Suffolk County Legislature isn’t on this ballot: no legislative districts appear on the Suffolk Board of Elections’ list. City and village offices keep their own schedules. Long Island’s villages hold their own elections at other times of year, so none is on this ballot.

Local names come from the county lists, which print them in capitals, so capitalization here may differ from the printed ballot. A local candidate is marked “Incumbent” only when an official page confirms it. Always check your official sample ballot.

How we find your ballot

  1. Your address goes to the Census Geocoder, which returns your 119th Congress district, your 2024 state legislative districts, your county and your city.
  2. We ask Google’s Civic Information API for the November 3, 2026 general election at your address.
  3. We match the researched ballot file to your districts. Statewide contests always apply. District contests match on district type and number. Supreme Court contests match on New York’s judicial district for your county (table below). Town contests match the Census county subdivision for your address (so the Town of Rye and the City of Rye stay separate), village and city contests match the incorporated place, and District Court contests match the towns and cities each court district covers. County legislative districts, New York City Council districts and New York City Civil Court municipal court districts aren’t in the Census lookup, so those contests in your county are listed under “may be on your ballot” with a district picker, unless Google lists your contest.
  4. Ballot proposals follow the same rules: New York City Charter proposals appear only on New York City ballots.
  5. When the researched file and Google list the same contest, we use the researched file’s candidates and identifiers and Google’s ballot order (only when the candidate names match exactly).
  6. If neither source has your ballot (for example, outside New York before Google loads the election), we show your current members of Congress, clearly labeled as officeholders and not as a ballot.

Contests are shown in the order offices usually appear on New York ballots: Governor and Lieutenant Governor, Comptroller, Attorney General, U.S. Senate, U.S. House, State Senate, Assembly, judicial offices, then county and city offices. An explicit ballot position from our sources overrides this.

How candidates are linked to records

A candidate’s records come from database IDs: a Bioguide ID (Congress), an FEC candidate ID, a NY Open Legislation member ID, and an Open States person ID.

  1. IDs listed in the researched ballot file are used first.
  2. Otherwise, for incumbents running for the seat they hold, we match on chamber, district and surname.
  3. Otherwise, we match on full name and state, which must match exactly one record.
  4. For federal money, a candidate without an FEC ID is matched by surname within the same state, office, district and cycle, and must match exactly one record.
  5. A sitting legislator whose record isn’t linked yet (for example, before the nightly data job has run) is labeled “Legislative record not linked yet,” not “no record.”

Each candidate’s Sources tab shows which rule linked each record.

The “At a glance” box

The box at the top of each candidate’s card has three lines, written by Ballot Scout from fixed sentence templates, the same way for every candidate. All numbers come from the Money and Votes tabs below it. There are no adjectives and no comparisons between candidates.

  • Money: for federal candidates, the total raised for this race through the latest FEC report and the share of money from individual donors that came in donations of $200 or less, plus outside spending reported for and against them, when there is any. For New York State candidates matched to a registered committee, the sum of itemized monetary contributions in the filings covered: schedules A, B and C (from individuals and partnerships, from corporations, and from all other contributors). In-kind contributions, transfers, loans, refunds, public matching funds and other receipts aren’t counted. If a filing’s schedules can’t be told apart reliably, the line points to the Money tab instead of giving a total. A running mate’s line points to the candidate for Governor, whose committee the ticket shares. Everyone else’s line says that no totals are shown and, where the Money tab has them, that it links to the official filings.
  • Positions: the first stated position on each issue you picked, from the Positions tab, or the reason none is shown.
  • Record: how many recorded votes on your issues the Votes tab lists, the date of the most recent one and the count for each issue, and, for a sitting Governor, how many bills on your issues they signed or vetoed.

Amounts are rounded: millions to one decimal place ($3.5 million), amounts from $10,000 to the nearest thousand ($812,000), and amounts from $1,000 to the nearest hundred ($9,500). The tabs show exact figures.

Positions on your issues

This tab lists what each candidate has said they would do on the issues you picked. Each position is a short paraphrase of the candidate’s own statement, written by Ballot Scout with AI assistance and then checked against its source. Every one links to where the candidate said it, with the type of source and its date, and any quote is the candidate’s exact words.

Sources

  • Used: the candidate’s campaign website; questionnaires they answered for nonpartisan voter guides; an officeholder’s official website; the candidate’s own social media accounts; and debates, interviews and news reports, only where the candidate is quoted directly.
  • Never used: what opponents, parties, endorsers or interest groups say about a candidate; ratings and scorecards; ads paid for by anyone else; opinion pieces; and anonymous or unsourced claims. Votes aren’t used here; they’re in the Votes tab.

Writing rules

The same rules apply to every candidate. These are checked in code, and a file that breaks any of them isn’t shown for anyone:

  • Each summary starts with a plain verb from a fixed list: Supports, Opposes, Proposes, Calls, Pledges, Promises, Says, Wants, Plans, Would, Favors, Seeks, Backs, Rejects, Argues, Advocates, Aims, Intends or Prioritizes.
  • No characterizing words, such as strong, bold, common-sense, radical, extreme, dangerous, failed, reckless, corrupt, champion, fight, proud, progressive, conservative, far-left, far-right, socialist, woke, great, terrible or crisis.
  • Quotes are no longer than 14 words.
  • Every summary links to its source.

Summaries also aim for 8 to 30 words and at most two positions per issue, and every quote is checked word for word against its source.

Equal effort across a race

Every candidate in a race is checked against the same kinds of sources: campaign website, nonpartisan questionnaires, official website, social media accounts, debates and interviews. Each card lists every source checked, including those where nothing was found. A candidate’s research is marked complete only when every check has been done, and a race’s positions are shown only when every candidate in it is complete. Until then the tab says so for everyone in the race, so you never see one candidate’s positions next to an opponent who hasn’t been researched. When a researched candidate has said nothing on your issues, the tab says that, lists the sources checked, and names the other issues they have stated positions on.

Judicial races

New York’s Rules Governing Judicial Conduct (22 NYCRR 100.5(A)(4)(d)) bar judicial candidates from making pledges or promises of conduct in office, or commitments on cases, controversies or issues likely to come before the court, that are inconsistent with the impartial performance of the office. So this guide doesn’t list positions for judicial races. Running mates’ positions are listed under the candidate for Governor.

Corrections

If a summary misstates what a candidate said, a link is broken, or a statement is missing, report an error with a link to the candidate’s own words. Campaigns can send corrections the same way. Every correction is checked against the same rules.

Which votes are included

We show votes on bills, not procedural votes. A vote is included when its bill’s issue matches one you picked (see the tables below).

U.S. Congress (Voteview roll calls, 118th and 119th Congresses by default)

  • Included by default: final passage (“On Passage,” “On the Bill,” “On the Joint Resolution,” “On Agreeing to the Resolution”), passage under suspension of the rules, conference reports, veto overrides, and motions to concur in the other chamber’s amendment (how a bill passes in its final form).
  • Included only with the “amendment and committee votes” switch: votes on amendments, except on issues defined by a published vote list (Israel and Palestine), which always show every listed vote, amendments included, so each member’s list is complete.
  • Never included: cloture, motions to proceed, table, recommit, reconsider or adjourn, the previous question, rules that set debate terms (“providing for consideration”), quorum calls, the Journal, nominations, and treaties.

Congress.gov’s own House roll-call endpoint covers only the House (and only recent Congresses), so we use Voteview for both chambers and link each vote to the official Clerk of the House or Senate record.

New York State

  • Senate: floor votes from Open Legislation. Committee votes appear with the switch.
  • Assembly: roll calls from Open States’ bulk export, classified by Open States as bill passage, amendment or veto override. Amendment votes appear with the switch.
  • Session covered: 2025–2026.

Vote labels are the official ones: Yea/Nay/Present/Not voting for Congress, and Aye/Nay/Excused/Absent/Abstained for New York.

Issue mapping tables

Each issue you can pick maps to official categories. Federal bills carry exactly one policy area assigned by the Congressional Research Service. New York bills are matched by the primary consolidated law they amend (Open Legislation’s “law section”), plus Open States subject tags when present. A bill can match more than one issue.

The Israel and Palestine vote list

Votes on military aid and arms for Israel are mostly amendments to, or parts of, large spending and defense bills whose policy area covers far more, so this issue isn’t matched by subject. It uses a published list of U.S. House roll calls chosen by a fixed rule, applied the same way to every vote and every member. Every vote links to the official Clerk of the House record, and every vote that was considered and not counted is listed with the reason.

What “What their policies did” shows

Only official evidence about bills the candidate sponsored, plus, for a sitting Governor, bills they signed or vetoed:

  • Congressional Budget Office cost estimates (title, description and link)
  • Congressional Research Service bill summaries, quoted verbatim (the enacted version when one exists)
  • Public law and New York chapter numbers
  • New York sponsor memos’ “Fiscal implications” section, quoted verbatim and labeled as the sponsor’s statement
  • The Governor’s approval and veto messages, quoted verbatim
  • A “Find GAO reviews” search link for each federal bill

When none of these exist, we say so plainly. We don’t estimate effects ourselves.

Money

  • Federal candidates: FEC totals for the 2025–2026 cycle (receipts, spending, cash on hand, and money from individuals, PACs and other committees, party committees and the candidate). “$200 and under” combines itemized contributions of $200 or less with unitemized contributions, as the FEC reports them. Donors’ employers are the FEC’s aggregation of itemized contributions by employer name as reported. Outside spending is described in its own section below.
  • State candidates in New York: each candidate is linked to their registered committee in the NYS Board of Elections filer data on data.ny.gov when exactly one active, state-level candidate committee matches the office, district and surname. Itemized transactions reported for 2025–2026 are then summed by schedule (A, B, C… as named in the filings). Each transaction number is counted once, so amended reports aren’t double-counted. Unitemized contributions aren’t itemized in the data, so totals can differ from the official reports’ summary pages. Before using the data, the nightly job checks that every column it needs exists, and it skips the source if any is missing.
  • Everyone else in New York (judicial candidates, running mates, and candidates without exactly one matching committee): links to the NYS Board of Elections disclosure search and the NYS Public Campaign Finance Board.

Outside spending and super PACs

Outside spending means independent expenditures: money a group spends on its own to support or oppose a candidate, such as ads and mailers, without coordinating with the campaign. Super PACs can raise unlimited amounts, but they can’t give money to candidates; they can only spend independently, and they must report every expenditure for or against a federal candidate to the FEC.

  • What’s shown: for every federal candidate on your ballot, the amounts spent supporting and opposing them in the 2023–24 and 2025–26 cycles, split by the spender’s FEC committee type: super PACs (independent expenditure-only committees, single-candidate super PACs, and hybrid PACs’ super PAC accounts), party committees, other PACs, and other groups and individuals. The largest spenders are listed by name with a link to their FEC filings. A candidate with nothing on record is shown as “None on record,” in the same layout as everyone else.
  • Which figures: totals come from the FEC’s processed reports (Schedule E, aggregated by candidate). 24- and 48-hour reports, which reach the FEC within a day or two of the spending, are summed separately (the latest version of each, without memo entries), because they usually reappear on the spender’s next regular report and adding the two would double count.
  • When: rebuilt nightly. Each figure shows the date of the data.
  • Matching: candidates are matched to FEC records the same way as the Money tab (exactly one record by surname within the state, office and district). A candidate who can’t be matched is labeled, not shown as “none.”
  • State and local races: independent expenditures for or against New York State and local candidates are reported to the NYS Board of Elections, not the FEC. Their Money tabs link to the Board’s search by candidate supported or opposed.

Known gaps

  • Votes cover recent sessions only: the 118th–119th Congresses and New York’s 2025–2026 session by default.
  • Issue matching depends on one policy area (federal) or one primary law (New York) per bill. Bills that touch several topics may not appear under every relevant issue.
  • New York’s Executive Law is too broad to map, so bills amending only that law (including the Human Rights Law) don’t match any issue. No New York consolidated law maps to immigration.
  • New York sponsor memos exist only for Senate bills in Open Legislation.
  • For bills the Governor signed without a separate message, the signer is the Governor named in that session’s approval and veto messages.
  • Assembly roll calls depend on Open States’ scrape of the Assembly website and may lag.
  • Local offices other than those in the researched file may be missing. One local ballot proposal outside New York City (Croton-on-Hudson Proposition One) was found on an official sample ballot; not every town’s and village’s sample ballot could be checked, so others may exist. Always check your official sample ballot.
  • The county candidate lists are labeled unofficial (Westchester) or subject to change (Nassau), and printed sample ballots couldn’t be checked, so a late substitution may not be reflected yet.
  • Records for county, town and village offices (votes, local laws, local campaign finance totals) aren’t covered yet.
  • The Israel and Palestine list covers House votes from January 3, 2023 through October 17, 2026. Two checks are still open and are listed with the vote list.
  • Outside spending covers federal races only, and the FEC’s processed totals can trail recent spending by weeks; 24- and 48-hour reports are shown separately to cover that gap.
  • New York State money figures are sums of itemized transactions, not the official report totals, and appear only for candidates matched to exactly one registered committee.
  • Stated positions are researched by hand, race by race, and only for races in the researched ballot file. A race shows no positions until every candidate in it has been fully researched.
  • Incumbency for judicial candidates isn’t verified in the researched ballot file, so no judicial candidate shows an “Incumbent” badge.
  • Outside New York, ballots depend on Google’s Civic Information API, and state legislative records aren’t included yet.

Privacy

Your address is sent in the body of one request, used only to look up your districts (with the Census Geocoder and Google’s Civic Information API), and never written to logs or storage. Short-lived in-memory caches are keyed by a one-way hash of the address. Candidate records are requested by candidate and issue only, so they contain nothing about you. No cookies, analytics or third-party scripts are used.

Report an error

If something is wrong or missing, tell us what you saw and the link to the record involved.

Errors in the underlying records can also be reported to the source: Congress.gov API issues, the FEC, NY Open Legislation, Open States, and Voteview.
